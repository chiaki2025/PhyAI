\section{The Meaning of Entropy}
From the previous chapter, we have been working with the term "entropy" in various situations: solids, ideal gases; but what exactly is entropy? Unlike other quantities such as volume ($V$), pressure ($P$), number of particles ($N$),...; it is not so easy to imagine an abstract and "invisible" thing like entropy ($S$). In this section, we discuss  an intuitive way of understanding entropy, without relying on formulas. Let's forget everything you have learned so far, and decorate a blank wall with a few pictures. Now I give you $5$ landscape pictures, and your mission is sticking them on my wall.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_1.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{$2$ possible picture arrangements}\label{fig:3_1}
\end{figure}
There are many possible ways of sticking pictures; I can not draw and count all of them here, but the number of possible arrangements is \textbf{not infinity}. You can think of entropy as the number of ways you can arrange the pictures on the wall. The more arrangements, the higher the entropy of our system (just consists a wall with five pictures). Obviously, if our wall is larger, we are more freely to stick our pictures wherever we want; and the entropy of our new system (again, larger wall with five picture) is greater than the smaller one.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_2.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Large system has more entropy than the small system}\label{fig:3_2}
\end{figure}
You can also increase the total entropy by sticking more and more pictures on the wall too. But now, do you see that if there are many different ways to arrange pictures, the wall will become even more cluttered? By the same logic, the higher the entropy, the more chaotic the system becomes? "Why is more cluttered? Bro, no matter the size of the wall, I always arrange everything parallel, like all pictures are in one row.". Yes, nothing is wrong; however, we need to note a subtle difference between \textbf{intentionally} arranging pictures in one row, and \textbf{randomly} sticking them on our wall. Apparently, when we concern how many possible arrangements, we are dealing with the \textbf{randomness}. Randomly sticking pictures on a small wall is less "chaotic" (or "messy") than on a larger one; since there are fewer ways to do so.

\begin{center}
	\textit{Entropy is a quantity that describes how chaotic (or messy) a system is.}
\end{center}
Now imagine you are sticking pictures on your wall $A$, nearby is my wall $B$. I ask if you could decorate my wall too, so our big wall would be so nice; and I assume that you agree.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_3.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{The total entropy of wall $A+B$ system}\label{fig:3_3}
\end{figure}
When you start moving pictures to my wall and sticking them on, we can see that our wall are "in contact" and "transferring" pictures with each other. This "interaction" process increases the total entropy of our system\footnote[1]{This conclusion is true, both in our common sense and in our calculations. We proved several special cases in the Chapter ~\ref{chap:the_second_law_of_thermodynamics}.}.
\begin{center}
	\textit{The interaction process increases the total entropy of a system.}
\end{center}
Inductively, we imagine the universe as a giant closed system, with many particles inside; and they are always exchanging "something"\footnote[2]{I do not know if the completely isolated object exists or not; its existence violates our laws of thermodynamics.} with each other, we can firmly state:
\begin{center}
	\textit{The total entropy of the universe is increasing.}
\end{center}
An ice cube is more "organized" than a cup of water, and vice versa. In the middle of winter, water is freezed. Does this phenomenon violate the second law of thermodynamics? Probably, because the liquid is now condensing (or "organizing") into a solid block of ice; obviously its entropy decreases!
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_4.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Water is freezed}\label{fig:3_4}
\end{figure}
Surprisingly, the answer is no. The water is cooled down since it \textbf{contacts} with the outside environment; they are \textbf{interacting}. Because the water's temperature is much higher than surrounding; so it gives up some of its energy, while the environment receives them. A decrease in energy leads to a decrease in entropy (particles become less mobile, thus making their movement more difficult), and vice versa. From the law of conservation of energy:
$$ \textbf{Energy released by water}=\textbf{Energy received by environment} $$
But:

$$ \textbf{Entropy "released" by water}\neq\textbf{Entropy "received" by environment}\footnote[3]{I use the double bracket here to emphasize the fact that "entropy" is not transferred in the way of energy or other physics quantities.  } $$
Entropy is closely related to energy, but they are not the same, and we do not have any law of conservation of entropy; so we have to be careful here. This result below may surprise you:
$$ \textbf{Entropy "released" by water}<\textbf{Entropy "received" by environment} $$
So the total entropy of our system (a cup of water and the outside environment) increases, because it gains more than losses.
\section{The Meaning of Temperature}\label{sec:the_meaning_of_temperature} % (fold)
In the conventional sense, a cup of boiling water has much higher temperature than its surrounding, so it will transfer some of its energy to surrounding environment (and conversely, the environment also receives that heat), according to the zeroth law of thermodynamics. As we discussed in the previous section:
\begin{center}
	\textit{Entropy is directly related to heat.}
\end{center}
And obviously, also by the law:
\begin{center}
	\textit{The amount of heat transferred between objects is directly linked to the temperature difference. }
\end{center}
The temperature itself is not so important than the \textbf{difference}. If our system only consists objects at same temperature, nothing is exchanged, everything is in equilibrium state. Only when $\Delta T>0$, some heat $Q$ is transferred, from the high temperature objects to the lower; which leads to the \textbf{total entropy of entire system} slowly \textbf{increases}. In our thinking line, we can imagine:
\begin{center}
	\textit{Entropy is something that deeply related not only heat, but also the temperature difference too.}
\end{center}
In this section, we develop the connection among three physical quantities: $ (\textbf{heat}, \textbf{energy}, \textbf{entropy}) $. Let's begin with a very classical problem: consider a system consists two Einstein solids $A$ ($N_{A}=40$) and $B$ $(N_{B}=60)$, and the total energy is $q=100$. Looking at table and graph, we find out some interesting observations here. I randomly pick an interval; $q_{A}$ is increasing $2$ units, from $q_{A}=20$ to $q_{A}=22$, its entropy is also increasing $\Delta S_{A}=40.32-38.19=2.13$ $(k_{B})$; meanwhile $S_{B}$ is decreasing $\Delta S_{B}=94.6-95.6=-1$ $(k_{B})$. We can see that:
$$ \Delta S_{\text{total}}=\Delta S_{A}+\Delta S_{B}=2.13+(-1)=1.13 \; (k_{B})$$
Again, the total entropy is increasing! Can I guess the slope of a tangent line at point $q_{A}=20$ is \textbf{steeper} than point $q_{B}=80$? Let's find out by doing some math here. Recall from our approximation formula:
$$ S=k_{B}[(q+N)\ln{(q+N)}-q\ln{q}-N\ln{N}]\Rightarrow \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\right)_{N}=k_{B} \left[\ln\left(1+\frac{N }{q}\right)\right] $$
Substituting our values yields:
$$ q_{A}=20, N_{A}=40\Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A}}{\partial q_{A}}= 1.09 k_{B}$$
$$ q_{B}=80, N_{B}=60\Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial q_{B}}=  0.55k_{B}$$
Here is a question, why is $\Delta S_{B}$ negative, but it still has positive slope? The answer is pretty simple, our graph perspective is from right to left, but calculation convention is from left to right. If the initial state of our system is $(q_{A}=0, q_{B}=100)$, then the total entropy is slowly increasing, until it reaches its \textbf{maximum value}, and stays still at equilibrium point. There is no chance for our system could fall out of its equilibrium state anymore,  after it reached \footnote[1]{Or more precisely, no matter where it begins, the probability of equilibrium state occurring is alwasy the highest. I recommend you should verify this fact by calculating the ratio: $$ \frac{P(q_{A}=40, q_{B}=60)}{P(q_{A}=45, q_{B}=55)}$$}.
\begin{definition}[Equilibrium Definition]
	Equilibrium state is the state which entropy reaches its \textbf{maximum value}, or equivalently: $$ \Delta S=0 $$
	\label{Equilibrium Definition}
\end{definition}
According the zeroth law of thermodynamics, the temperature of solid $A$ and $B$ are equal if and only if they are in equilibrium, that means $S_{\text{total}}$ is now maximum. Obviously, equilibrium point is the local maxima, so its tangent line is now \textbf{straight}. We take the first derivative with respect to $q_{A}$ :
$$ \frac{\partial S_{\text{total}}}{\partial q_{A}}=0\Rightarrow \frac{\partial (S_{A}+S_{B})}{\partial q_{A}}=0 \Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial q_{A}}=-\frac{\partial S_{B} }{\partial q_{A}}$$
A subtle observation is that a decrease in $q_{A}$ corresponds to an increase in $q_{B}$:
$$ \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial q_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial q_{B}} $$
This equation makes perfect sense to our intuition, look at previous calculation: the slope of $S_{A}$ graph is much steeper than $S_{B}$, then it gradually decreases, while the slope of $S_{B}$ slowly increases until they are \textbf{equal}. That is an equilibrium moment. Now if our system is not in equilibrium, I assume:
$$ \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial q_{A}}>\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial q_{B}} $$
To ensure the second law of thermodynamics, as you can see here, the entropy of solid $A$ increases \textbf{faster} than the rate of entropy decrease of solid $B$. That means solid $A$ receives energy released from solid $B$; and now we can conclude that \textbf{the temperature of solid $A$ is lower than solid $B$.} Before introducing the canonical definition of temperature, let me define a new "energy" quantity unit $\varepsilon\footnote[1]{The unit of $\varepsilon$ is (eV), which is the kinetic energy of an electron that has been accelerated through a voltage different of $1$V.}$:
$$ U=\varepsilon.q $$
Apparently, we can see that:
$$ \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial q_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial q_{B}}\Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial U_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial U_{B}} $$
\begin{definition}[The Third Definition of Temperature]
	The temperature is defined as:
	\label{The Third Definition of Temperature}
	$$\frac{1 }{T}=\frac{\partial S }{\partial U}\; (1/K) $$
\end{definition}
We now eventually understood why the unit of entropy is (J/K) (thanks to the unit of $k_{B}$ constant). Plugging it to the definition above, the energy part (J) disappears, so only (1/K) left.
