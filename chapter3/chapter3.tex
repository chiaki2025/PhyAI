\section{The Meaning of Entropy}
From the previous chapter, we have been working with the term "entropy" in various situations: solids, ideal gases; but what exactly is entropy? Unlike other quantities such as volume ($V$), pressure ($P$), number of particles ($N$),...; it is not so easy to imagine an abstract and "invisible" thing like entropy ($S$). In this section, we discuss  an intuitive way of understanding entropy, without relying on formulas. Let's forget everything you have learned so far, and decorate a blank wall with a few pictures. Now I give you $5$ landscape pictures, and your mission is sticking them on my wall.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_1.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{$2$ possible picture arrangements}\label{fig:3_1}
\end{figure}
There are many possible ways of sticking pictures; I can not draw and count all of them here, but the number of possible arrangements is \textbf{not infinity}. You can think of entropy as the number of ways you can arrange the pictures on the wall. The more arrangements, the higher the entropy of our system (just consists a wall with five pictures). Obviously, if our wall is larger, we are more freely to stick our pictures wherever we want; and the entropy of our new system (again, larger wall with five picture) is greater than the smaller one.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_2.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Large system has more entropy than the small system}\label{fig:3_2}
\end{figure}
You can also increase the total entropy by sticking more and more pictures on the wall too. But now, do you see that if there are many different ways to arrange pictures, the wall will become even more cluttered? By the same logic, the higher the entropy, the more chaotic the system becomes? "Why is more cluttered? Bro, no matter the size of the wall, I always arrange everything parallel, like all pictures are in one row.". Yes, nothing is wrong; however, we need to note a subtle difference between \textbf{intentionally} arranging pictures in one row, and \textbf{randomly} sticking them on our wall. Apparently, when we concern how many possible arrangements, we are dealing with the \textbf{randomness}. Randomly sticking pictures on a small wall is less "chaotic" (or "messy") than on a larger one; since there are fewer ways to do so.

\begin{center}
	\textit{Entropy is a quantity that describes how chaotic (or messy) a system is.}
\end{center}
Now imagine you are sticking pictures on your wall $A$, nearby is my wall $B$. I ask if you could decorate my wall too, so our big wall would be so nice; and I assume that you agree.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_3.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{The total entropy of wall $A+B$ system}\label{fig:3_3}
\end{figure}
When you start moving pictures to my wall and sticking them on, we can see that our wall are "in contact" and "transferring" pictures with each other. This "interaction" process increases the total entropy of our system\footnote[1]{This conclusion is true, both in our common sense and in our calculations. We proved several special cases in the Chapter ~\ref{chap:the_second_law_of_thermodynamics}.}.
\begin{center}
	\textit{The interaction process increases the total entropy of a system.}
\end{center}
Inductively, we imagine the universe as a giant closed system, with many particles inside; and they are always exchanging "something"\footnote[2]{I do not know if the completely isolated object exists or not; its existence violates our laws of thermodynamics.} with each other, we can firmly state:
\begin{center}
	\textit{The total entropy of the universe is increasing.}
\end{center}
An ice cube is more "organized" than a cup of water, and vice versa. In the middle of winter, water is frozen. Does this phenomenon violate the second law of thermodynamics? Probably, because the liquid is now condensing (or "organizing") into a solid block of ice; obviously its entropy decreases!
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_4.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Water is frozen}\label{fig:3_4}
\end{figure}
Surprisingly, the answer is no. The water is cooled down since it \textbf{contacts} with the outside environment; they are \textbf{interacting}. Because the water's temperature is much higher than surrounding; so it gives up some of its energy, while the environment receives them. A decrease in energy leads to a decrease in entropy (particles become less mobile, thus making their movement more difficult), and vice versa. From the law of conservation of energy:
$$ \textbf{Energy released by water}=\textbf{Energy received by environment} $$
But:

$$ \textbf{Entropy "released" by water}\neq\textbf{Entropy "received" by environment}\footnote[3]{I use the double bracket here to emphasize the fact that "entropy" is not transferred in the way of energy or other physics quantities.  } $$
Entropy is closely related to energy, but they are not the same, and we do not have any law of conservation of entropy; so we have to be careful here. This result below may surprise you:
$$ \textbf{Entropy "released" by water}<\textbf{Entropy "received" by environment} $$
So the total entropy of our system (a cup of water and the outside environment) increases, because it gains more than losses.
\section{The Meaning of Temperature}\label{sec:the_meaning_of_temperature} % (fold)
In the conventional sense, a cup of boiling water has much higher temperature than its surrounding, so it will transfer some of its energy to surrounding environment (and conversely, the environment also receives that heat), according to the zeroth law of thermodynamics. As we discussed in the previous section:
\begin{center}
	\textit{Entropy is directly related to heat.}
\end{center}
And obviously, also by the law:
\begin{center}
	\textit{The amount of heat transferred between objects is directly linked to the temperature difference. }
\end{center}
The temperature itself is not so important than the \textbf{difference}. If our system only consists objects at same temperature, nothing is exchanged, everything is in equilibrium state. Only when $\Delta T>0$, some heat $Q$ is transferred, from the high temperature objects to the lower; which leads to the \textbf{total entropy of entire system} slowly \textbf{increases}. In our thinking line, we can imagine:
\begin{center}
	\textit{Entropy is something that deeply related not only heat, but also the temperature difference too.}
\end{center}
In this section, we develop the connection among three physical quantities: $ (\textbf{heat}, \textbf{energy}, \textbf{entropy}) $. Let's begin with a very classical problem: consider a system consists two Einstein solids $A$ ($N_{A}=40$) and $B$ $(N_{B}=60)$, and the total energy is $q=100$. I randomly pick an interval; $q_{A}$ is increasing $2$ units, from $q_{A}=20$ to $q_{A}=22$, its entropy is also increasing $\Delta S_{A}=40.32-38.19=2.13$ $(k_{B})$; meanwhile $S_{B}$ is decreasing $\Delta S_{B}=94.6-95.6=-1$ $(k_{B})$. We can see that:
$$ \Delta S_{\text{total}}=\Delta S_{A}+\Delta S_{B}=2.13+(-1)=1.13 \; (k_{B})$$
Again, the total entropy is increasing! Can I guess the slope of a tangent line at point $q_{A}=20$ is \textbf{steeper} than point $q_{B}=80$? Let's find out by doing some math here. Recall from our approximation formula:
$$ S=k_{B}[(q+N)\ln{(q+N)}-q\ln{q}-N\ln{N}]\Rightarrow \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\right)_{N}=k_{B} \left[\ln\left(1+\frac{N }{q}\right)\right] $$
Substituting our values yields:
$$ q_{A}=20, N_{A}=40\Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A}}{\partial q_{A}}= 1.09 k_{B}$$
$$ q_{B}=80, N_{B}=60\Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial q_{B}}=  0.55k_{B}$$
Here is a question, why is $\Delta S_{B}$ negative, but it still has positive slope? The answer is pretty simple, our graph perspective is from right to left, but calculation convention is from left to right. If the initial state of our system is $(q_{A}=0, q_{B}=100)$, then the total entropy is slowly increasing, until it reaches its \textbf{maximum value}, and stays still at equilibrium point. There is no chance for our system could fall out of its equilibrium state anymore,  after it reached \footnote[1]{Or more precisely, no matter where it begins, the probability of equilibrium state occurring is always the highest. I recommend you should verify this fact by calculating the ratio: $$ \frac{P(q_{A}=40, q_{B}=60)}{P(q_{A}=45, q_{B}=55)}$$}.
\begin{definition}[Equilibrium Definition]
	Equilibrium state is the state which entropy reaches its \textbf{maximum value}, or equivalently: $$ \Delta S=0 $$
	\label{Equilibrium Definition}
\end{definition}
According the zeroth law of thermodynamics, the temperature of solid $A$ and $B$ are equal if and only if they are in equilibrium, that means $S_{\text{total}}$ is now maximum. Obviously, equilibrium point is the local maxima, so its tangent line is now \textbf{straight}. We take the first derivative with respect to $q_{A}$ :
$$ \frac{\partial S_{\text{total}}}{\partial q_{A}}=0\Rightarrow \frac{\partial (S_{A}+S_{B})}{\partial q_{A}}=0 \Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial q_{A}}=-\frac{\partial S_{B} }{\partial q_{A}}$$
A subtle observation is that a decrease in $q_{A}$ corresponds to an increase in $q_{B}$:
$$ \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial q_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial q_{B}} $$
This equation makes perfect sense to our intuition, look at previous calculation: the slope of $S_{A}$ graph is much steeper than $S_{B}$, then it gradually decreases, while the slope of $S_{B}$ slowly increases until they are \textbf{equal}. That is an equilibrium moment. Now if our system is not in equilibrium, I assume:
$$ \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial q_{A}}>\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial q_{B}} $$
To ensure the second law of thermodynamics, as you can see here, the entropy of solid $A$ increases \textbf{faster} than the rate of entropy decrease of solid $B$. That means solid $A$ receives energy released from solid $B$; and now we can conclude that \textbf{the temperature of solid $A$ is lower than solid $B$.} Before introducing the canonical definition of temperature, let me define a new "energy" quantity unit $\varepsilon\footnote[1]{The unit of $\varepsilon$ is (eV), which is the kinetic energy of an electron that has been accelerated through a voltage different of $1$V.}$:
$$ U=\varepsilon.q $$
Apparently, we can see that:
$$ \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial q_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial q_{B}}\Leftrightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A} }{\partial U_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial U_{B}} $$
\begin{definition}[The Third Definition of Temperature]
	The temperature is defined as:
	\label{The Third Definition of Temperature}
	$$\frac{1 }{T}=\left(\frac{\partial S }{\partial U}\right)_{N,V}\; (1/K) $$
\end{definition}
We now eventually understood why the unit of entropy is (J/K) (thanks to the unit of $k_{B}$ constant). Plugging it to the definition above, the energy part (J) disappears, so only (1/K) left.
\\
\indent Now I can derive the equipartition theorem (Theorem ~\ref{The Equipartition Theorem}) for the two simplest cases: Einstein solid and ideal monatomic gases. Let's begin with the entropy formula of a solid at high temperature (Subsection ~\ref{sub:_n_and_q_are_large_values}):
$$ S=k_{B}N \ln{\frac{eq }{N}}=k_{B}N \ln{\frac{eU }{\varepsilon N}}\Rightarrow \frac{1 }{T}=\left(\frac{\partial S }{\partial U}\right)_{N,V}=\frac{k_{B}N }{U}\Rightarrow U=k_{B}NT $$
Is there any conflict here? Since we already know that any solid has $6$ degrees of freedom, its internal energy must be: $$ U=\frac{f }{2}k_{B}NT=3k_{B}NT\quad(\text{Corollary ~\ref{Total Internal Energy}})$$
Here is the subtle observation, the key difference is how the quantity $N$ defined:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		U&=k_{B}NT\quad\text{($N$ is \textbf{the number of chemical bonds inside solid})} \\
		U&=3k_{B}NT\quad\text{($N$ is \textbf{the number of particles inside solid})}
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
Inside crystal lattice, each particle has $3$ chemical bonds, so our proof is correct:
$$ N_{\textbf{bonds}}=3N_{\textbf{particles}} $$
For convention, we agree that $N$ is \textbf{the number of chemical bonds inside Einstein solid}; so the heat capacity of solid at constant volume is:
$$ C_{V}=\left(\frac{\partial U }{\partial T}\right)_{N,V}=k_{B}N  $$
For ideal monatomic gas, from Theorem ~\ref{The Entropy of Ideal Gas}, take the partial derivative with respect to $U$, we obtain:
$$ \frac{1 }{T}=\left(\frac{\partial S }{\partial U}\right)_{N,V}=\frac{3 }{2}Nk_{B}\frac{1 }{U} \Rightarrow U=\frac{3 }{2}Nk_{B}T $$
Again, the equipartition theorem still holds true. Similarly, the heat capacity at constant volume is: $$ C_{V}=\left(\frac{\partial U }{\partial T}\right)_{N,V}=\frac{3 }{2}Nk_{B} $$

\section{The Third Law of Thermodynamics}
From the definition of temperature (Definition ~\ref{The Third Definition of Temperature}):
$$ \frac{1 }{T}=\left(\frac{\partial S }{\partial U}\right)_{N,V} $$
An infinitesimally small change in entropy is equivalent to:
$$ dS=\frac{dU }{T} $$
We assume that our system does not do any kind of works:
$$ dS=\frac{dU }{T}=\frac{Q+W }{T}=\frac{Q }{T}=\frac{C_{V}dT}{T}\Rightarrow \Delta S=\int_{T_{i} }^{T_{f}}\frac{C_{V}dT }{T}  $$
This seems like a very easy way; you can calculate the difference in entropy of any system with constant volume, but here is the tricky part. I set the initial temperature $T_{i}=0$ (K):
$$ \Delta S=S_{f}-S(0)=\int_{0 }^{T_{f}}\frac{C_{V}dT }{T}=C_{V}.\lim_{t\to 0^+}(\ln{T_{f}}-\ln{t})  $$
This integral is divergence, and might be $\Delta S\to+\infty$, do you think that makes sense? The answer is no, there is no way entropy (or any other physical quantities) can be infinite. To ensure convergence, our mathematics indicates condition:
$$ T\to 0\;\text{(K)}\Rightarrow C_{V}\to 0\;\text{(J/K)} $$
How about $S(0)$? Intuitively, we imagine the state where everything is "frozen", and due to equipartition theorem (Theorem ~\ref{The Equipartition Theorem}), the internal energy is equal zero, leads to number of units energy is also zero; and there is \textbf{only one} configuration that satisfies $q=0$.
$$ S=k_{B}\ln\Omega=k_{B}\ln{1}=0\;(\text{J/K}) $$
\begin{theorem}[The Third Law of Thermodynamics]
	\label{The Third Law of Thermodynamics}
	At absolute zero temperature a system settles into its unique lowest-energy state:
	$$ \lim_{T\to 0}S=S(0)=0\;\text{(J/K)} $$
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[The Macroscopic View of Entropy]
	For any system, the difference in entropy can easily be determined as follows:
	\label{The Macroscopic View of Entropy}
	$$    dS=\frac{dU }{T}\Rightarrow\Delta S=\int_{i}^{f}\frac{dU }{T}\;(J/K)$$
\end{theorem}
\begin{exmp} $100$ grams of water is heated from $298$K to $328$K at \textbf{constant volume} condition; given the specific heat of water is $c_{V}=4.13$ (kJ/kg.K).
	\\ The amount of increased entropy is:
	$$ \Delta S=\int_{i }^{f}\frac{dU }{T}=\int_{T_{i} }^{T_{f}}\frac{C_{V}dT}{T}= C_{V}.\ln{\frac{T_{f} }{T_{i}}}=m.c_{V}.\ln{\frac{T_{f} }{T_{i}}}=0.1.4.13.0.096=0.0413\;(\text{kJ/K}) $$
	100 grams of water at $328$K (55$^{\circ}$C) has:
	$$ \frac{\Omega_{f} }{\Omega_{i}}= \exp \left(\frac{\Delta S }{k_{B}}\right)= \exp{(3.10^{24})}\;(\text{times})$$
	more "chaotic" than at $298$K (25$^{\circ}$C).
\end{exmp}

\subsection{Thermal Equilibrium}
Now it is time to focus on the most common types of equilibrium in thermodynamics systems. We discussed thermal equilibrium in the Chapter ~\ref{chap:the_zeroth_law_of_thermodynamics} of this book, but I want to recall some important properties here, and we will solve a simple example directly related to this phenomenon.
\begin{definition}[Definition of Thermal Equilibrium]
	\label{Definition of Thermal Equilibrium}
	Systems are in thermal equilibrium when \textbf{their temperatures} are the same.
\end{definition}
From Definition ~\ref{The Third Definition of Temperature}:
$$ \frac{1 }{T}= \left(\frac{\partial S }{\partial U}\right)_{N,V}  $$
\begin{exmp}
	A $50$ grams copper sphere at $100^{\circ}$C is dropped into a cup containing $100$ grams of water at $25^{\circ}$C. Given $c_{V,\ce{H2O}}=4.13$ (kJ/kg.K) and $c_{P,\ce{Cu}}=386\footnote[1]{I could not find any detailed information about $c_{P,\ce{Cu}}$ value on the internet; but that is understandable because we usually measure $c_{V}$ of liquids and gases, $c_{P}$ of solids. Maintaining constant volume condition (for liquids and gases) and constant pressure (for solids) is much easier when conducting any type of measurement.}$ (J/kg.K); show that the total entropy of system is increasing.
	\\ \indent Using the same technique from the last example in Chapter ~\ref{chap:the_zeroth_law_of_thermodynamics}, we obtain the equilibrium (final) temperature is $T_{f}=301.34K$ ($28.34^{\circ}$C).
	\begin{equation*}
		\begin{split}
			\Delta S_{\ce{H2O}}&=\int_{i }^{f}\frac{dU }{T}=C_{V}.\int_{T_{i} }^{T_{f}} \frac{dT }{T}=m_{\ce{H2O}}.c_{V,\ce{H2O}}.\int_{T_{i} }^{T_{f}} \frac{dT }{T}=0.1.4.13.10^{3}.\ln{\frac{301.34}{298}}=4.6\text{(J/K)}\\
		\end{split}
	\end{equation*}
	Here is the subtle part of this problem, how do we know $\Delta S_{\ce{Cu}}$? We only know its $c_{P,\ce{Cu}}$ value, and can not directly apply the formula above. When looking at our equation, I notice that I can remove something:
	$$ dU=Q+W=Q+P\Delta V=Q+P(\beta.V.\Delta T)=Q+P(\beta.\rho.m.\Delta T)\footnote[2]{$\beta$:  coefficient of volume expansion (1/K), $\rho$: density (kg/$\text{m}^3$). The product result is small enough (you should verify yourself) to be neglected.  } \approx Q$$
	The difference in entropy is:
	$$ \Delta S_{\ce{Cu}}=\int_{i }^{f}\frac{dU }{T}=C_{P}\int_{i }^{f}\frac{dT }{T}=m_{\ce{Cu}}.c_{P,\ce{Cu}}\int_{T_{i} }^{T_{f}}\frac{dT }{T}=0.05.386.\ln{\frac{301.34 }{373}}=-4.1\text{ (J/K)}    $$
	Finally, we now can conclude that the total entropy is increasing, as expected:
	$$ \Delta S=\Delta S_{\ce{H2O}}+\Delta S_{\ce{Cu}}=4.6-4.1=+0.5\;\text{(J/K)} $$
\end{exmp}

\subsection{Mechanical Equilibrium}\label{sec:mechanical_equilibrium} % (fold)

\begin{definition}[Definition of Mechanical Equilibrium]
	\label{Definition of Mechanical Equilibrium}
	Systems are in mechanical equilibrium when \textbf{their pressures} are the same.
\end{definition}
The novelty in this definition is that we will redefine the concept of pressure in terms of temperature and entropy. Let's consider a simple model: two ideal gas $A$ and $B$, divided by a sliding partition. They can exchange energy, but not particles. To make it easier to visualize, I have drawn Figure ~\ref{fig:2_11} and Figure ~\ref{fig:2_12}. On the peak of $S_{\text{total}}$, you can see that both partial derivatives with respect to $U_{A}$ and $V_{A}$ are equal zeros.
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		\frac{\partial S_{\text{total}} }{\partial U_{A}}&=0\Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A}    }{\partial U_{A}}=-\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial U_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial U_{B}}\\
		\frac{\partial S_{\text{total}} }{\partial V_{A}}&=0\Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A}    }{\partial V_{A}}=-\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial V_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial V_{B}}\\
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
Now we form the conditions for mechanical equilibrium:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{cases}
		\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{A}}{\partial U_{A}}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial U_{B}}\quad\text{(Thermal Equilibrium)} \\
		\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{A}}{\partial V_{A}}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial V_{B}}                                  \\
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
Obviously, if a system is in mechanical equilibrium; it \textbf{must be in thermal equilibrium}.
\begin{definition}[The Second Definition of Pressure]
	\label{The Second Definition of Pressure}
	The pressure of any system is defined as:
	$$ P=T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{N,U}\quad(\text{Pa}) $$
\end{definition}
This definition looks very weird, but somehow it still makes perfect sense. Let me explain the way I understand it. Consider the ideal gas law:
$$ PV=Nk_{B}T \Rightarrow P=\frac{Nk_{B}T }{V}$$
The product on the right side is \textbf{proportional} to the internal energy $U$, so I can rewrite our equation to:
$$ P\propto \frac{U }{V} \Rightarrow P=\text{constant}.\left(\frac{\Delta U }{\Delta V}\right)$$
Look carefully to the units both sides, you can see the relationship between energy and volume is described by pressure quantity. Intuitively, if you increase the volume with fixed internal energy, pressure decreases; and similarly, if you hold the volume and increase internal energy (by adding more gas, or heat the system), pressure increases. According to the Theorem ~\ref{The Macroscopic View of Entropy}:
$$ dU=TdS $$
Substituting to the previous equation, and choose $T$ to be the constant:
$$ P=T\left(\frac{\partial S }{\partial V}\right) $$
\subsection{Diffusive Equilibrium}
Before explaining what these terms are, let me introduce you to some examples of "diffusion" first: mixing tea and milk together, or gases mixing phenomenon (Figure ~\ref{fig:2_14}). I often think that diffusion is closely related to mixing.
\begin{definition}[Definition of Diffusion]
	\label{Definition of Diffusion}
	Diffusion is the net movement of anything generally from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.
\end{definition}
But you should note that these phenomenons are not examples of diffusion: mixing $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{Cl2}$, or any kind of chemical reaction.
\begin{definition}[Definition of Diffusive Equilibrium]
	\label{Definition of Diffusive Equilibrium}
	Systems are in diffusive equilibrium when \textbf{their chemical potentials} are the same.
\end{definition}
What is chemical potential? I will start with a simple container, contains $2$ ideal gases $A$ and $B$, separated by a partition. Then I pull out the partition, the gases start mixing together, and when they reach equilibrium state:

\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		\frac{\partial S_{\text{total}} }{\partial U_{A}}&=0\Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A}    }{\partial U_{A}}=-\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial U_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial U_{B}}\\
		\frac{\partial S_{\text{total}} }{\partial N_{A}}&=0\Rightarrow \frac{\partial S_{A}    }{\partial N_{A}}=-\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial N_{A}}=\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial N_{B}}\\
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
Now we form the conditions for diffusive equilibrium:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{cases}
		\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{A}}{\partial U_{A}}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial U_{B}}\quad\text{(Thermal Equilibrium)} \\
		\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{A}}{\partial N_{A}}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial N_{B}}                                  \\
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
\begin{definition}[Definition of Chemical Potential]
	\label{Definition of Chemical Potential}
	The chemical potential of any system is defined as:
	$$ \mu=-T\left(\frac{\partial S }{\partial N}\right)_{U,V}\quad\text{(eV)} $$
	Particles tend to flow from the system with \textbf{higher $\mu$ into the system with lower $\mu$.}
\end{definition}
A very strange quantity (again?), but it is very useful in describing how fluids flow. For example, if I mix cold tea and hot milk together, I can calculate their chemical potentials and know whether the milk tends to flow into the tea or in the reverse direction. I often relate it to electrical potential, both tell us the tendency to flow, from high to low potential. What does the negative sign do? Let's find out; first I define a new quantity $u$:
$$ u=\frac{\partial U }{\partial N} $$
The quantity $u$ describes the rate of change of $U$; if $u$ is high, it means that $U$ is increasing rapidly and particles \textbf{tends to flow into} the fluid we are considering. If I mix fluids $A$ and $B$ together, known that $u_{A}>u_{B}$, then I can conclude that the flow of particles is from $B$ to $A$. There is nothing wrong here, except I feel a little but uncomfortable; perhaps I should add a negative sign to ensure our calculating direction is not reversed?
$$ -u_{B}>-u_{A}\Leftrightarrow \text{ Flowing direction: $B\to A$} $$
My small modification works very well, I want to connect it with other quantities:
$$ -u=-\frac{\partial U }{\partial N}=-T\left(\frac{\partial S }{\partial N}\right)=\mu $$
That is how I imagine the way of chemical potential $\mu$ was defined. But unlike electrical potential, $\mu_{A}$ and $\mu_{B}$ \textbf{constantly change}, until they are equal in equilibrium state.
\subsection{The Thermodynamics Identity}\label{sub:the_thermodynamics_identity} % (fold)
For any changing in entropy in any thermodynamics process:
$$ dS= \frac{dU }{T}+\frac{PdV }{T}-\frac{\mu dN }{T}$$
\begin{theorem}[The Thermodynamics Identity]
	\label{The Thermodynamics Identity}

	$$ dS= \frac{dU }{T}+\frac{PdV }{T}-\frac{\mu dN }{T}\Leftrightarrow dU=TdS-PdV+\mu dN$$
\end{theorem}
This identity will play crucial role in later chapters.

\section{Heat Engines and Refrigerators}\label{sec:heat_engine} % (fold)
\footnote[1]{This section is unrelated to the rest of this book; so you may omit or postpone it if you wish.} In the final section of this chapter, we will study two other versions of the second law of thermodynamics.
\begin{definition}[Heat Engine]
	A heat engine is a device that extracts energy from its environment in the form of heat and does useful work.
\end{definition}
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_6.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Simple schematic of a heat engine}\label{fig:3_6}
\end{figure}
The engine receives heat energy $Q_{H}$ from hot reservoir $T_{H}$, converts it into useful work $W$, and some of energy is lost to the cold reservoir $T_{L}$. We define a new quantity:
\begin{definition}[Efficiency of Heat Engine]
	$$ \varepsilon=\frac{|W| }{|Q_{H}|}=\frac{|Q_{H}|-|Q_{L}| }{|Q_{H}|} $$
\end{definition}
A version of the second law applied to heat engines is:
\begin{center}
	\textit{It is impossible to create an ideal heat engine, a type of engine that has $\varepsilon=100\%$.}
\end{center}
\begin{definition}[Refrigerator]
	A refrigerator is a device that uses work in order to transfer energy from a low-temperature reservoir to a high-temperature reservoir.
\end{definition}
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_7.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Simple schematic of a refrigerator}\label{fig:3_7}
\end{figure}
This engine receives work $W$ to transfer heat $Q_{L}$ from cold reservoir $T_{L}$ to hot reservoir $T_{H}$. We define a new quantity:
\begin{definition}[Coefficient of Peformance]
	$$ K=\frac{|Q_{L}| }{|W|} $$
\end{definition}
A version of the second law applied to refrigerators is:
\begin{center}
	\textit{It is impossible to create an ideal refrigerator, a type of engine that has $K=+\infty$.}
\end{center}
The Carnot cycle is the simplest thermodynamics process capable of producing work. For simplicity, I will only consider the Carnot cycle for heat engine (for refrigerator, the cycle proceeds in the reverse direction).
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter3/3_8.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{A very simple Carnot cycle}\label{fig:3_8}
\end{figure}
A very interesting consequence of the second law is that, since a perfect Carnot engine can not exist, the Carnot cycle is, in essence, \textbf{irreversible}\footnote[1]{Since this is not our main topic, I briefly introduce some important concepts here. For more details (or explanations) regarding the relationship between entropy and engines, you should refer to sections 20-2, 20-3, "Fundamentals of Physics" or chapter 4, "An Introduction to Thermal Physics".}.
