\section{Magnetic Dipoles and Diamagnetism}\label{sec:magnetic_dipoles_and_diamagnetic} % (fold)
\subsection{Magnetic Dipoles}
I think this final chapter is the most subtle in the book. From the first chapter until now, we have only been working with the assumption that all matter is made up of randomly moving "particles" without considering their structure. Do you remember Atomic Theory from the previous chapter (Theorem ~\ref{Atomic theory})? We treat it as the foundation (or as I said, an axiom). But we are having trouble explaining the essence of magnetism, our old theory, simply reaches its own limit. Now we must use a bit of the Bohr's model to keep everything on track. Let's begin with a simple experiment, imagine you have a single loop of wire between two magnetic poles. Inside the loop, a current flows counterclockwise. Applying left-hand rule, you can draw the direction of Lorentz force, which causes the loop to rotate:
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_1.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{A loop inside magnetic field $\vec{B}$}\label{fig:6_1}
\end{figure}
A rectangular loop has size $a\times b$, under the net torque:
$$ \vec{\tau}_{\text{net}}=\sum\vec{\tau}=\sum\vec{R}\times\vec{F} =\sum\vec{R}\times i\dot(\vec{L}\times\vec{B})$$
I define $\vec{n}$ as the normal vector of the loop, and an angle $\theta=(\vec{n},\vec{B})$, because both torques vectors are in the same direction, we obtain:
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_2.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Both torque vectors in pointing out from the plane of paper}\label{fig:6_2}
\end{figure}
The scalar $\tau_{net}$ value is:
$$ \tau_{\text{net}}=2.\frac{b }{2}.iaB\sin\theta=iab\sin\theta.B=iA\sin\theta.B $$
I define a new vector $\vec{\mu}$ quantity, called \textbf{magnetic dipole moment}, same direction to normal vector $\vec{n}$, and has magnitude: $$ |\vec{\mu}|=iA =iab$$
Rewriting the net torque equation yields:
$$ \vec{\tau}_{\text{net}}=\vec{\mu}\times \vec{B} $$
\begin{definition}[Magnetic Moment Definition]
	The \textbf{magnetic moment} (or magnetic dipole moment) is a vector quantity that determines the magnitude of torque in a given magnetic field:
	$$ \vec{\tau}=\vec{\mu}\times  \vec{B}$$
\end{definition}
Each angle $\theta$ (position), it is characterized by a \textbf{potential energy value}. I randomly choose 2 angles $\theta_{i}$ and $\theta_{f}$:
$$ W=-\Delta U=\int_{\theta_{i}}^{\theta_{f}}\tau d\theta=-|\vec{\mu}|.|\vec{B}|.(\cos\theta_{f}-\cos\theta_{i})=-(U_{f}-U_{i})$$
I assign the potential energy value to angle $\theta$: $$ U(\theta)=-\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{B} $$
From here we have a very important result: a magnetic dipole has its lowest energy when its vector $\vec{\mu}$ is lined up with magnetic field $\vec{B}$, and vice versa.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.666666\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_3.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Highest and lowest potential energy value}\label{fig:6_3}
\end{figure}
To flip an arrow (magnetic moment vector) down, we must supply $2\mu B$ external energy units.

Inside Bohr's atomic model, each electron has its own unique quantity called \textbf{spin}. Spin determines the direction of an electron's motion (clockwise or counterclockwise). If a material has perfectly matched "loops"\footnote[1]{Chemistry textbooks only go as far as explaining how molecules exhibit (or lack) diamagnetic property by drawing \textbf{molecules orbital diagram}; and now we fully understand why "paired" electrons can cause diamagnetism.} (electron orbits around nucleus), it tends to be \textbf{repelled from stronger magnetic field}; and we call such a material \textbf{diamagnetic}\footnote[2]{Most biological bodies are diamagnetic materials, meaning that we can make them float in mid-air using a sufficiently strong magnetic field: \href{https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=diamagnetism+frog}{Frog floats in mid-air experiment}.}.
\section{Paramagnetism}\label{sec:paramagnetism} % (fold)
\subsection{The Macroscopic View of Paramagnetism}\label{sub:macroscopic_view_of_paramagnetism} % (fold)
If a material does not have any matched "loops" (or "unpaired" electrons), it tends to be \textbf{attracted from stronger magnetic field}, thanks to the effect of magnetic moment $\vec{\mu}$\footnote[1]{A very well-known example of a paramagnetic material is liquid oxygen, in contrast to liquid nitrogen, a diamagnetic material. You should draw their molecules orbital diagram to see the difference.}.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_7.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{The macroscopic view of paramagnetic material}\label{fig:6_7}
\end{figure}
An external magnetic field $\vec{B}$ immediately magnetizes the material, forcing the random vectors to align; but when the magnetic field is removed, the material returns to its original state. We define the concept of \textbf{magnetization} of a sample, which represents the density of magnetizing dipole moment per unit volume.
\begin{definition}[Magnetization Definition]
	$$ M=\frac{\text{measured magnetic moment }}{V} $$
\end{definition}
In 1985, Pierre Curie discovered experimentally the relationship:
$$ M\propto \frac{B }{T} $$
This proportional relationship seems like very obvious; the higher the temperature, the harder the alignment of magnetic moment vectors, and vice versa.
% subsection Macroscopic view of Paramagnetism (end)
\subsection{The Microscopic View of Paramagnetism}
\subsubsection{Qualitative Perspective}
To examine the paramagnetic material, and explain some surprising phenomenons, we have to equip statistical tool. For simplicity, I restrict our concern to \textbf{two-state paramagnet}. Two-state paramagnet are the materials in which the magnetic moment vector can only point either up or down.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_8.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Two-state paramagnet}\label{fig:6_8}
\end{figure}
If I denote $N$ as "the number of", the multiplicity and entropy of the system are:
$$ \Omega (N_{\uparrow})=\binom{N}{N_{\uparrow}}\Rightarrow S=k_{B}\ln\Omega=k_{B}\ln\binom{N}{N_{\uparrow}}$$
The internal energy is determined by:
$$ U=\mu B(N_{\downarrow}-N_{\uparrow})=\mu B(N-2N_{\uparrow}) $$
I neglect the constant $B$ in the magnetization formula, and redefine the $M$ quantity as:
$$ M=\mu (N_{\uparrow}-N_{\downarrow})=-\frac{U }{B}$$
Before we look for an analytical solution, let's consider the visual representation for these things. We all know how binomial function work, so I can draw a simple table with $4$ columns:
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_9.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Relationship between $N_{\uparrow}, S,U,M$}\label{fig:6_9}
\end{figure}
If our two-state paramagnet starts moving from state $N_{\uparrow}=0$ to $N_{\uparrow}=N$, I can plot the graph of $(U,S)$:
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_10.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{The graph of $(U,S)$}\label{fig:6_10}
\end{figure}
What can we see here? Recall the definition of temperature (Definition ~\ref{The Third Definition of Temperature}), we determine the temperature of our system at $4$ special labeled points. Wow, this is the first time we observe the \textbf{negative temperature} appears; and seems like these states $U\to 0^+$ and $U\to 0^-$ are very unstable, corresponds to $N_{\upward}\to (N/2)^-$ and $N_{\upward}\to (N/2)^+$. Maybe the "exact state", where $S$ is exactly equal to zero, can not happen. I might guess the graphs of $(U,T)$ and $(T,M)$ should look like this:
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_11.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{$(U,T)$ and $(T,M)$ graphs}\label{fig:6_11}
\end{figure}
By sketching our data on graphs, the fact that $M\propto \displaystyle\frac{1 }{T}$ still holds, and confirm Currie's empirical result we discussed above.
\subsubsection{Quantitative Perspective}
From the entropy formula and Stirling's weak approximation, I calculate the entropy $S$ of any huge paramagnet:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		S&=k_{B}\ln\binom{N}{N_{\uparrow}}=k_{B}.\frac{N!}{N_{\uparrow}!(N-N_{\uparrow})!}\\&\approx k_{B}[N\ln{N}-N_{\uparrow}\ln{N_{\uparrow}}-(N-N_{\uparrow})\ln{(N-N_{\uparrow})}]
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
The temperature function is:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		\frac{1}{T}&=\left( \frac{\partial S }{\partial U}\right)_{N,V,B}=\frac{\partial S }{\partial N_{\uparrow}}.\frac{\partial N_{\uparrow}}{\partial U}=\frac{\partial S }{\partial N_{\uparrow}}.\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N_{\uparrow}}\right)^{-1}\\
		&=k_{B}[-\ln{N_{\uparrow}}-1-(-\ln{(N-N_{\uparrow})}-1)].(-2\mu B)^{-1}\\
		&=k_{B}\ln\left(\frac{N_{\downarrow}}{N_{\uparrow}}\right).(-2\mu B)^{-1}\\
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
I rewrite $N_{\uparrow}$ and $N_{\downarrow}$ functions with respect to $U$:
\begin{equation*}
	U=\mu B(N-2N_{\uparrow})\Leftrightarrow
	\begin{cases}
		N_{\uparrow}=\frac{N-U/\mu B}{2}   \\
		N_{\downarrow}=\frac{N+U/\mu B}{2} \\
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
We obtain:
$$ \frac{1 }{T}=\frac{k_{B}}{2\mu B}\ln\left(\frac{N-U/\mu B}{N+U/\mu B}\right) $$
I rearrange some terms here:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		\exp{\left(\frac{2\mu B}{k_{B}T}\right)}&=\frac{N-U/\mu B}{N+U/\mu B}\\
		\Leftrightarrow \frac{N\mu B -U }{N\mu B +U}&=\exp{\left(\frac{2\mu B}{k_{B}T}\right)}\\
		\Leftrightarrow N\mu B - U&=(N\mu B+U)\exp{\left(\frac{2\mu B}{k_{B}T}\right)}\\
		\Leftrightarrow N\mu B \left[1-\exp{\left(\frac{2\mu B}{k_{B}T}\right)}\right]&=U\left[1+\exp{\left(\frac{2\mu B}{k_{B}T}\right)}\right]\\
		\Leftrightarrow U&=N\mu B \left[\frac{1-\exp{(2\mu B/k_{B}T)}}{1+\exp{(2\mu B/k_{B}T)}}\right]
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
From the definition of $\tanh{x}$ function:
$$ \tanh{x}=\frac{\sinh{x}}{\cosh{x}}=\frac{1/2(e^{x}-e^{-x})}{1/2(e^{x}+e^{-x})}=\frac{1-e^{-2x}}{1+e^{-2x}} $$
Finally obtain the function of $U$, with respect to $T$ and constants $\mu$ and $B$:
$$ U=-N\mu B\tanh{\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_{B}T}\right)} $$
Similarly, for the magnetization function $M$:
$$ M=-\frac{U}{B}=N\mu\tanh{\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_{B}T}\right)} $$
The numerical function strongly verify our intuition is correct.
\subsubsection{Partition Function Perspective}
I will now use a completely different tool to determine $U$ and $M$ functions of two-state paramagnet. Consider a piece of two-state paramagnetic material under an external magnetic source $\vec{B}$, contains \textbf{only one magnetic moment vector}. Applying Boltzmann distribution (Theorem ~\ref{Boltzmann Distribution}), I obtain some important results:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		Z&=\sum_{s}\exp(-\beta E(s))=\exp(-\beta E_{\uparrow})+\exp(-\beta E_{\downarrow})=\exp(\beta\mu B )+\exp(-\beta\mu B )=2\cosh{(\beta \mu B)}\\
		\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}&=\frac{1 }{Z}\exp{(-\beta E_{\uparrow})}=\frac{1 }{2\cosh(\beta \mu B)}\exp{(\beta \mu B)}\\
		\mathcal{P}_{\downarrow}&=\frac{1 }{Z}\exp{(-\beta E_{\downarrow})}=\frac{1 }{2\cosh(\beta \mu B)}\exp{(-\beta \mu B)}\\
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
The average energy of our system can be determined in two ways:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		\overline{E}&=\sum_{s}\mathcal{P}(s)E(s)=\mathcal{P}_{\uparrow}E_{\uparrow}+\mathcal{P}_{\downarrow}E_{\downarrow}=\frac{-\mu B \exp{(\beta \mu B)}+\mu  B\exp{(-\beta \mu B)}}{2\cosh(\beta \mu B)}\\
		&=\frac{-\mu B[2\sinh{(\beta \mu B)}]}{2\cosh{(\beta \mu B)}}=-\mu B\tanh{(\beta \mu B)}
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
Or:
$$ \overline{E}=-\frac{1 }{Z}\frac{\partial Z }{\partial \beta}=-\frac{1 }{2\cosh(\beta\mu B)}.\mu B\sinh{(\beta \mu B)}=-\mu B \tanh{(\beta \mu B)}$$
If our system is very huge and contains $N$ magnetic moment vector, then we have:
$$ U=N\overline{E}=-N\mu B\tanh{(\beta \mu B)}=-N\mu B\tanh{\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_{B}T}\right)} $$

\section{Ferromagnetism and Ising Model}\label{sec:ferromagnetism_and_ising_model} % (fold)
In an ideal paramagnet, magnetic dipoles respond only to the external magnetic field $\vec{B}$ and the dipoles are \textbf{independent}. But in some materials (like iron), these moment vectors \textbf{influence} each other, even in the absence of an external magnetic field. We call that material \textbf{ferromagnetic}. Together, these parallel vector clusters form \textbf{domains} within the ferromagnet. For a very basic material, its vectors can only point up or down, we represent all its state on a square lattice.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_12.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Represent ferromagnet by using Ising model}\label{fig:6_12}
\end{figure}
Let $N$ be the total number of dipoles, and $s_{i}$ be the current state of the $i$th dipole. For convention, $s_{i}=+1$ (vector is pointing up), $s_{i}=-1$ (vector is pointing down). The energy due to the interaction of a pair of neighboring dipoles will be $-\varepsilon$ when they are \textbf{parallel}, and $+\varepsilon$ when they are \textbf{antiparallel}. The total energy of the system from all the \textbf{nearest-neighbor interaction} is:
$$ U=-\varepsilon\sum_{\{i,j\}} s_{i}s_{j}$$
The partition function $Z$ is: $$ Z=\sum\exp{(-\beta U)} $$
\subsection{Analytical Solution in One Dimension}
Consider a system containing only one neighboring pair, I denote $\uparrow\uparrow$ for parallel state, and $\uparrow\downarrow$ for antiparallel state.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_13.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{$Z$ function for only one neighboring pair}\label{fig:6_13}
\end{figure}
For a row of $N$ dipoles, we can approx the $Z$ function:
$$ Z\approx (2\cosh(\beta\varepsilon))^{N}=2^{N}\cosh^{N}{(\beta\varepsilon)} $$
The average energy of our system is:
$$ \overline{U}=-\frac{1 }{Z}\frac{\partial Z }{\partial \beta}=-N\varepsilon\tanh{(\beta\varepsilon)} $$
\subsection{The Mean Field Approximation}
Obviously, ferromagnet is much complicated than a row of $N$ dipoles. The Ising model now is extended to two, or three dimensions; so we have to develop a new approximation tool to study it.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_14}
	\end{center}
	\caption{An example of $2\times 2$ Ising model}\label{fig:6_14}
\end{figure}
I randomly pick a dipole $i$ inside crystal lattice, represent it on a grid with $n$ neighbors around. If the vector $i$ points up (or down), we obtain two energy functions:
$$ U_{\uparrow}=-\varepsilon\sum_{\text{neighbors}}s_{i}s_{\text{neighbor}}=-\varepsilon n\overline{s} $$
$$ U_{\downarrow}=-\varepsilon\sum_{\text{neighbors}}s_{i}s_{\text{neighbor}}=\varepsilon n\overline{s} $$
for $\overline{s}$ is \textbf{the average neighboring states} around dipole $i$.
The partition function of dipole $i$ is:
$$ Z_{i}=\sum\exp{(-\beta U)} =2\cosh{(\beta \varepsilon n\overline{s})}$$
I reuse this result to determine the \textbf{average dipole $i$ state}:
$$ \overline{s_{i}}=\sum s\mathcal{P}(s)=\frac{1 }{Z_{i}}[(+1)\exp{(\beta \varepsilon n \overline{s})}+(-1)\exp{(-\beta\varepsilon n\overline{s})}]=\tanh{(\beta\varepsilon n\overline{s})} $$
Here is a subtle insight; if $n$ is very large (dipole $i$ is surrounded by many neighbors), then $\overline{s_{i}}\approx\overline{s}$. Rewriting this relation yields:
$$ \overline{s}\approx \tanh{(\beta\varepsilon n \overline{s})} $$
Wow, that is how we roughly approx the average state of any ferromagnetic material.
\subsection{Metropolis Algorithm}
Given an Ising model size $N\times N$, we count a total of $2^{N}$ possible states. How can we find the most stable state (or the state in thermal equilibrium)?
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_15.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Finding the state in thermal equilibrium}\label{fig:6_15}
\end{figure}
To make it easier to visualize, I always imagine this phenomenon like placing a small ball on op of the hill (initial state), and no matter what height I place it at, it will always roll back to the bottom (final state). Similarly, for random Ising model in any energy state; its moment vectors always seek to interact internally to find its own thermal equilibrium state. But unlike rolling ball (which can easily be described by a potential energy function), we determine the most probable state of Ising model using the \textbf{Boltzmann factor}:
$$ \mathcal{P}(s)\propto \exp(-\beta E(s)) $$
% \usepackage{algorithm,algorithmicx,algpseudocode}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\floatname{algorithm}{Procedure: Finding the thermal equilibrium state}
	\algrenewcommand\algorithmicrequire{\textbf{Input: A random $N\times N$ Ising model}}
	\algrenewcommand\algorithmicensure{\textbf{Output: }}
	\caption{}\label{alg:1}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\State \text{Generate a random $N\times N$ Ising model}
		\State \text{Randomly choose a dipole $i$}
		\If{$\Delta U\leq 0$}
		\State{\text{Flip dipole $i$}}
		\Else
		\State\text{$P$(\text{Flip dipole} $i$)$=\exp{(-\beta \Delta U})$}
		\EndIf
		\State\text{Repeat the process}
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\\To fully understand how it works, I will try to explain using the example of a rolling ball (again). Why  when $\Delta U$ is negative, I can certainly conclude that our ball will roll downward (or dipole $i$ should be flipped)? The probability of rolling down is:
$$ P(1\to 2)=\frac{\mathcal{P}(s_{2})}{\mathcal{P}(s_{1})}=\exp(-\beta\Delta U)>1 $$
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_16.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{A ball is placed on the top of the hill}\label{fig:6_16}
\end{figure}
Otherwise, when $\Delta U$ is positive, the situation is reversed. From the algorithm, the probability of our ball rolling upward is very small, \textbf{but not zero}:
$$ P(2\to 1)=\frac{\mathcal{P}(s_{1})}{\mathcal{P}(s_{2})}=\exp(-\beta\Delta U) $$
\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{~/PhyAI/chapter6/6_17.png}
	\end{center}
	\caption{A ball is placed on the bottom of the hill}\label{fig:6_17}
\end{figure}
My basic example confirm that this procedure is in perfect agreement with Boltzmann distribution. Here is the algorithm run for actual Ising model:
\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\floatname{algorithm}{Metropolis Algorithm: Searching the equilibrium state of Ising model}
	\algrenewcommand\algorithmicrequire{\textbf{Input: A random $N\times N$ Ising model}}
	\algrenewcommand\algorithmicensure{\textbf{Output: }}
	\caption{}\label{alg:2}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\State \text{Generate a random $N\times N$ Ising model}
		\For {$\text{interation}=1$ \text{to} $\text{interation}=10000$}
		\State \text{Randomly choose a dipole $(i,j)$}
		\State \text{$U= $\Call{DeltaU}{$i,j$}}
		\If {$U\leq 0$}
		\State{Flip dipole $(i,j)$}
		\Else
		\If{$\text{rand}(0,1)<\exp(-\beta U)$} \Comment{Monte Carlo Simulation}
		\State{Flip dipole $(i,j)$}
		\EndIf
		\EndIf
		\EndFor
		\Function{DeltaU}{$i,j$}
		\State$U=2*\text{state}(i,j)*(\text{top}+\text{bottom}+\text{left}+\text{right})$
		\State{\text{Return}} $U$
		\EndFunction
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

	\end{center}
	\caption{Metropolis algorithm illustration}\label{fig:6_19}
\end{figure}
